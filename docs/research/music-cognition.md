# Music cognition and semiotics for Batuque

Research notes on how music evokes emotion, groove, catchiness, arrangement and semiotics, turned into design rules for the shows. Date: 2026-11-17.

Scope: evidence for the scene order, melodies, arrangement and beats of an instrumental web show of
Pernambuco rhythms.

## 1. How music evokes emotion

### Confirmed

- **Eight mechanisms (BRECVEMA).** Music can induce emotion through Brain stem reflex, Rhythmic
  entrainment, Evaluative conditioning, Contagion, Visual imagery, Episodic memory, Musical
  expectancy and Aesthetic judgment. Aesthetic judgment was added to explain admiration and awe.
  Juslin 2013, "From everyday emotions to aesthetic emotions: Towards a unified theory of musical
  emotions", _Physics of Life Reviews_ 10(3), 235–266.
  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.plrev.2013.05.008
- **Expectation drives emotion (ITPRA).** Huron describes five response systems: Imagination,
  Tension, Prediction, Reaction, Appraisal. A correct prediction is rewarded. Uncertainty before
  an event creates tension. A fast reaction response can be negative while a slower appraisal is
  positive, and this contrast can heighten pleasure ("contrastive valence"). Huron 2006, _Sweet
  Anticipation: Music and the Psychology of Expectation_, MIT Press.
  https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/6575.001.0001
- **Meaning from inhibited tendencies.** Meyer's older thesis: emotion arises when an expected
  continuation is delayed or blocked. Meyer 1956, _Emotion and Meaning in Music_, University of
  Chicago Press (publisher record not re-read in this pass).
- **Anticipation itself is rewarding.** PET and fMRI showed dopamine release in the striatum at
  peak pleasure. The caudate was more active while anticipating the peak; the nucleus accumbens
  was more active at the peak. Salimpoor et al. 2011, "Anatomically distinct dopamine release
  during anticipation and experience of peak emotion to music", _Nature Neuroscience_ 14,
  257–262. https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.2726
- **One code for voice and music.** A meta-analysis of 104 speech studies and 41 music-performance
  studies found that similar acoustic cue changes express the same emotions in both channels.
  Juslin & Laukka 2003, "Communication of emotions in vocal expression and music performance:
  Different channels, same code?", _Psychological Bulletin_ 129(5), 770–814.
  https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.129.5.770
- **Cue weights in controlled synthesis.** Across 200 synthesized excerpts, the ranking of cue
  influence was mode, tempo, register, dynamics, articulation, timbre. Effects were mostly linear
  and additive, with almost no cue interactions.
  - Happy: major, faster, higher.
  - Sad: minor, slower, darker timbre.
  - Peaceful: slower, softer, duller timbre; timbre was the strongest cue.
  - Scary: minor, lower register, louder, brighter timbre.

  Eerola, Friberg & Bresin 2013, "Emotional expression in music: Contribution, linearity, and
  additivity of primary musical cues", _Frontiers in Psychology_ 4:487.
  https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00487
- **Structural cues review.** Gabrielsson & Lindström name mode, tempo, dynamics, articulation,
  timbre and phrasing as the most potent cues. Gabrielsson & Lindström 2010, "The role of
  structure in the musical expression of emotions", in Juslin & Sloboda (eds.), _Handbook of Music
  and Emotion_, Oxford UP, 367–400. Catalog record: https://zfl.vufindnet.de/zfl/Record/a0037139
  (summary via Eerola et al. 2013).
- **Trance is cultural, not acoustic.** Rouget concludes that no universal law links music and
  trance. The link depends on the cultural system of meaning. Rouget 1985, _Music and Trance_,
  University of Chicago Press (translated from the 1980 French edition).
  https://chicagoreference.com/ucp/books/book/chicago/M/bo5956162.html
- **Deep listening resembles ritual trance.** Becker 2004, _Deep Listeners: Music, Emotion, and
  Trancing_, Indiana University Press (publisher details confirmed, content from the publisher
  description).
- **The sad-music pleasure hypothesis did not hold.** Huron proposed that prolactin makes sad
  music pleasurable (Huron 2011, _Musicae Scientiae_ 15(2),
  https://doi.org/10.1177/102986491101500202). Ladinig et al. found no prolactin rise (2019/2021,
  _Musicae Scientiae_). Huron acknowledged the negative result. Do not build on it.

### Unverified (recalled cue profiles, Juslin & Laukka 2003, summary table)

| Emotion    | Tempo | Loudness       | Timbre                                     | Articulation and attack                | Pitch                  |
| ---------- | ----- | -------------- | ------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Anger      | fast  | high           | bright, sharp (much high-frequency energy) | staccato, abrupt attacks               | high level, rising     |
| Sadness    | slow  | low            | dull                                       | legato, slow attacks, final ritardando | low, narrow, falling   |
| Tenderness | slow  | low            | soft, dull                                 | legato, slow attacks                   | low                    |
| Happiness  | fast  | medium to high | medium-bright                              | staccato, fast attacks                 | high, rising, variable |

Check this table against the paper before using it in public copy. The secondary sources found in
this pass agree on sadness and the arousal grouping (anger high, tenderness low).

### Inferences for the feelings in the show

- **Sensuality** has no established cue profile in this literature. _Inference:_ combine the
  tenderness profile (slow, legato, soft timbre, low register) with strong low-frequency groove,
  which keeps a body pull (see section 2).
- **Euphoria** maps to high arousal and positive valence: fast, major, high register, bright, with
  an anticipated peak (Salimpoor 2011).
- **Trance** cannot rest on sound alone (Rouget 1985). _Inference:_ the show can afford trance
  through long, low-variation repetition and entrainment (BRECVEMA "R"), but the cultural frame,
  maracatu's ritual context, does the rest. Copy should not claim the music causes trance.
- **Saudade** is not a studied category. _Inference:_ model it as sadness cues (slow, minor or
  modal, falling contour) mixed with tenderness and the memory mechanism (BRECVEMA "E").

## 2. Groove and beats

### Confirmed

- **Groove is a measurable construct.** Groove is the urge to move with a positive feeling.
  High-groove music elicits spontaneous movement, and sensorimotor coupling predicts groove
  ratings. Janata, Tomic & Haberman 2012, "Sensorimotor coupling in music and the psychology of
  the groove", _Journal of Experimental Psychology: General_ 141(1), 54–75.
  https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024208
- **Inverted U of syncopation.** With funk drum breaks, medium syncopation gave the highest urge to
  move and pleasure; low and high gave less. Witek, Clarke, Wallentin, Kringelbach & Vuust 2014,
  "Syncopation, body-movement and pleasure in groove music", _PLOS ONE_ 9(4), e94446.
  https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0094446
- **Microtiming does not create groove by itself.** Keil argued that "participatory discrepancies"
  (small timing deviations) give music its power (Keil 1987, _Cultural Anthropology_ 2(3),
  275–283, https://doi.org/10.1525/can.1987.2.3.02a00010). Experiments found the opposite for
  short patterns: systematic microtiming lowered groove ratings, except for a jazz shuffle, and
  more so for experts (Davies, Madison, Silva & Gouyon 2013, _Music Perception_ 30(5), 497–510,
  https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2013.30.5.497). Quantized drum patterns were rated highest
  (Frühauf, Kopiez & Platz 2013, _Musicae Scientiae_ 17(2), 246–260,
  https://doi.org/10.1177/1029864913486793). Senn et al. 2016 supported neither strong position
  (_Frontiers in Psychology_, https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01487).
- **Bass carries the timing.** Timing deviations were detected better in the lower of two tones
  (larger mismatch negativity), and tapping followed the lower stream. Hove, Marie, Bruce &
  Trainor 2014, "Superior time perception for lower musical pitch explains why bass-ranged
  instruments lay down musical rhythms", _PNAS_ 111(28), 10383–10388.
  https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1402039111
- **Lower bass gives more groove.** Lower bass and bass drum frequencies raised groove ratings and
  made tapping more stable. Stupacher, Hove & Janata 2016, "Audio features underlying perceived
  groove and sensorimotor synchronization in music", _Music Perception_ 33(5).
  Preprint: https://escholarship.org/uc/item/2sw3r2dw
- **Repetition is the core of music.** Margulis argues repetition shifts attention from the next
  note to the larger shape and invites participation. Margulis 2014, _On Repeat: How Music Plays
  the Mind_, Oxford University Press (publisher details confirmed, argument from descriptions).

### Inferences

- The alfaia, zabumba, surdo or 808 is where the listener locks the pulse. Keep it on time and low.
- Human feel can live in velocity and in upper parts (agbê, ganzá, triangle) rather than in large
  timing offsets of the low part.

## 3. Catchiness and memorability

### Confirmed

- **Earworm features.** About 3,000 survey participants named earworms; 100 of them were compared
  with 100 matched tunes on 83 melodic features. Earworms had more common global contours, less
  common gradients between turning points (an unusual interval or leap) and faster tempos.
  Jakubowski, Finkel, Stewart & Müllensiefen 2017, "Dissecting an earworm: Melodic features and
  song popularity predict involuntary musical imagery", _Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and
  the Arts_ 11(2), 122–135. https://doi.org/10.1037/aca0000090
- **Hooks are built from repetition, variation and production.** Burns's typology covers textual,
  non-textual, performance and production hooks, made with repetition, variation and modulation.
  Burns 1987, "A typology of 'hooks' in popular records", _Popular Music_ 6(1), 1–20.
  https://doi.org/10.1017/S0261143000006577
- **Repetition makes things feel musical.** Margulis 2014, as above.

### Inferences

- A hook for an instrumental show is a short motif (one to two bars) with a familiar arch or wave
  contour and one distinctive leap or rhythm (Jakubowski 2017).
- Call and response is native to coco and maracatu. _Inference:_ it supplies repetition with
  variation for free. No study in this pass measured its effect on memory.

## 4. Arrangement and production

### Confirmed

- **Staggered entrances raise groove.** Music whose parts entered one by one produced more tapping
  and head movement and higher groove ratings than music where all parts started together. More
  parts also raised groove. Musical training did not change the effect. Hurley, Martens & Janata
  2014, "Spontaneous sensorimotor coupling with multipart music", _Journal of Experimental
  Psychology: Human Perception and Performance_ 40(4), 1679–1696.
  https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037154
- **Breakdown, build-up, drop.** In a club setting with motion capture, movement fell during the
  breakdown, rose during the build-up and peaked after the drop. Dancers rated the build-up and
  drop as most pleasurable. Solberg & Jensenius 2017, "Pleasurable and intersubjectively embodied
  experiences of electronic dance music", _Empirical Musicology Review_ 11(3–4), 301–318.
  https://doi.org/10.18061/emr.v11i3-4.5023 (DOI form not re-checked; record:
  https://duo.uio.no/handle/10852/55312). Critique: only four tracks; average movement over a
  track did not rise, because each peak follows a dip (Witek & Vuust, same journal).
- **Spectral flux and energy variability relate to groove** (Stupacher et al., earlier
  "Decrypt the groove" version; correlations r ≈ .34 to .57). Treat the numbers as preliminary.

### Inferences

- Frequency space: give each layer its own band. Bass and drum low, melody mid-high, shakers high.
  This follows Hove 2014 (low for time, high for melody) but is standard mixing practice, not a
  tested rule here.
- A click grid is acceptable for the low part (Frühauf 2013, Davies 2013). Small, consistent swing
  belongs to the style when the style has it (forró, frevo).

## 5. Semiotics applied to music

### Confirmed

- **Tagg's sign types.** In _Music's Meanings_ a museme relates to what it connotes in three ways:
  as an anaphone (sonic, tactile, kinetic or social, through "gestural interconversion"), as a
  style flag (style indicator: the "home style"; genre synecdoche: "referring to other music and
  its connotations"), or as an episodic marker ("signifying start, end or bridge"). Tagg 2012
  (e-book) / 2013 (print), _Music's Meanings: A Modern Musicology for Non-Musos_, Mass Media Music
  Scholars' Press. https://www.tagg.org/mmmsp/NonMusoInfo.htm
  - Unverified: the exact definitions of each anaphone type. The usual reading is: sonic imitates a
    sound, kinetic imitates a movement, tactile imitates a touch or texture.
- **Peirce for music.** Turino applies icon (resemblance), index (co-occurrence, experience) and
  symbol (convention) to music, emotion and identity. Indices tie a sound to the people and events
  where it was heard. Turino 1999, "Signs of imagination, identity, and experience: A Peircian
  semiotic theory for music", _Ethnomusicology_ 43(2), 221–255. https://doi.org/10.2307/852734
  (DOI not re-checked).
- **Topic theory.** Ratner named topics: conventional styles (march, hunt, pastoral, dance types)
  that carry known associations. Agawu formalized topics as signs that interact with beginning,
  middle and end structure. Ratner 1980, _Classic Music: Expression, Form, and Style_, Schirmer
  (not re-read). Agawu 1991, _Playing with Signs: A Semiotic Interpretation of Classic Music_,
  Princeton UP. https://press.princeton.edu/node/63496
- **Tarasti's modalities.** Tarasti draws on Greimas. In an essay from the same project: "being"
  matches consonance and rest, "doing" matches dissonance and tension, "becoming" matches the
  transition between them. Tarasti 1994, _A Theory of Musical Semiotics_, Indiana UP. Essay:
  https://scholarworks.iu.edu/iuswrrest/api/core/bitstreams/127e3bab-ecc6-4c15-af4d-ff4f666ed584/content

### Inferences per rhythm (our reading, not the sources')

| Rhythm     | Style flag (what must be present)      | Anaphone to use                          | Peirce                                   |
| ---------- | -------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| Maracatu   | alfaias, gonguê, agbê                  | kinetic: the cortejo walk                | index of the nação and the street        |
| Coco       | ganzá, pandeiro, foot-stomp pattern    | kinetic and tactile: the tamanco on wood | index of the roda                        |
| Frevo      | fast brass-like lines, marcha pulse    | kinetic: the passista's leaps            | symbol of Carnival                       |
| Manguebeat | alfaia under distorted bass            | sonic: grit, city noise                  | genre synecdoche of maracatu inside rock |
| Forró      | zabumba, triangle, accordion-like line | kinetic: the couple's two-step           | index of São João                        |
| Brega      | crooner-like melody, soft keyboard     | tactile: smooth, close                   | symbol of the radio ballad               |
| Bregafunk  | tamborzão-like kick, sub bass          | kinetic: the passinho                    | index of the periphery party             |

Topic theory suggests each scene should open with its style flag before anything else, so the
genre is recognized at once (Agawu 1991; Tagg's style indicator).

## Design rules for Batuque

Each rule names its source. Rules marked _inference_ are our extrapolation.

### Scene order (script)

1. **Alternate arousal; never place two peaks side by side.** Put a low-arousal scene (brega
   romântico, saudade) before the high-arousal finale (frevo, euphoria). _Inference_ from contrastive
   valence (Huron 2006) and the breakdown-then-drop effect (Solberg & Jensenius 2017).
2. **End on the highest-arousal, major, fast, high-register scene.** Frevo fits: fast, bright,
   high. Eerola et al. 2013 (happy cues); Salimpoor 2011 (anticipated peak).
3. **Put trance (maracatu) where the viewer has time.** Long, steady repetition, no sudden changes.
   Margulis 2014; Rouget 1985 (do not promise trance in copy).
4. **Give each scene a clear opening marker.** A short break or a solo style-flag instrument signals
   "new scene". Tagg 2013 (episodic marker).

### Beats

5. **Medium syncopation only.** One or two off-beat accents per bar on top of a clear pulse. Avoid
   both a square grid in every part and constant syncopation. Witek et al. 2014.
6. **Keep the low part strictly on the grid; put human feel in the high parts.** Use velocity
   variation and a small, consistent swing where the style has one. Hove et al. 2014; Davies et al.
   2013; Frühauf et al. 2013.
7. **Tune the lowest drum low and give it a clear attack.** This applies to the alfaia, zabumba and
   bregafunk kick. Stupacher et al. 2016.

### Arrangement and density

8. **Enter instruments one at a time.** Order: pulse (low drum), timeline (gonguê, ganzá, agbê),
   melody last. Each entry is a step of the wheel. Hurley et al. 2014.
9. **Use a density curve: build, full, drop out, return.** Thin the texture to one or two parts
   for a break of 2 to 4 bars, then return all at once on the downbeat. Solberg & Jensenius 2017;
   Huron 2006 (tension then resolution).
10. **Make the build-up predictable.** The listener should know a return is coming, with a rising
    line, added layers or a snare roll, so the anticipation reward fires. Salimpoor 2011.
11. **One layer per frequency band.** Low = pulse, mid = harmony, high = melody and shakers.
    _Inference_ from Hove et al. 2014.

### Melody and hook

12. **Write the hook as a short motif (1 to 2 bars) with a familiar arch or wave contour plus one
    unusual leap or rhythmic kink.** Jakubowski et al. 2017.
13. **State the hook early, repeat it at least three times, vary it once (end note, rhythm or
    register), and bring it back unchanged at the end.** Burns 1987; Margulis 2014.
14. **Use call and response between two voices** (for example, a lead line answered by brass or
    accordion timbre). _Inference_; native to coco and maracatu, not tested for memory in this pass.
15. **Pick emotion cues by weight: mode first, tempo second, register third, then dynamics,
    articulation and timbre.** Eerola et al. 2013.
    - Anger: fast, loud, bright, staccato.
    - Sadness and saudade: slow, minor or modal, low, legato, falling phrase ends.
    - Tenderness and sensuality: slow, soft, dark timbre, legato, low register, with a deep bass
      groove for sensuality.
    - Euphoria: fast, major, high, bright.
      Juslin & Laukka 2003 (profiles unverified, see section 1).

### Semiotics

16. **Every scene must carry its genre's style flag from bar one**, as listed in the table in
    section 5. Tagg 2013; Agawu 1991.
17. **Match the background animation to the music's kinetic anaphone.** Examples: walking pulse for
    maracatu, stomp hits for coco, leaps for frevo, couple sway for forró. Tagg 2013 (kinetic
    anaphone); _inference_ for visuals.
18. **Use tension and rest as the visual rhythm too.** Motion and dissonance build ("doing"), then
    settle on the cadence ("being"). Tarasti 1994; _inference_ for visuals.

## Gaps and cautions

- The Juslin & Laukka 2003 cue table and Tagg's anaphone definitions were not read in full.
- Not re-read in this pass: Meyer 1956, Ratner 1980, the Turino 1999 DOI and the Solberg DOI.
- No study found on emotion cues for sensuality or saudade.
- No study found on the groove of Pernambuco rhythms specifically. Witek's evidence comes from funk
  drum breaks, Solberg's from EDM.
- Groove effects are averages. Expert listeners judge microtiming more strictly (Davies et al. 2013).
